FlyRank ML internship — capstone research paper

A field guide to content archetypes

What clustering reveals about page behavior that a single traffic tier can't

Abstract Content teams often triage pages by one number: how much traffic they get. This paper asks whether that single dimension hides real, actionable structure. Using K-Means clustering on 30,000 pseudonymized content pages from FlyRank's search performance data, we identify seven recurring behavioral archetypes — from high-traffic pages losing ground fast to low-demand pages that are, oddly, well positioned. Two of the seven archetypes surface direct review priorities that a traffic-only baseline would have buried inside a single "excellent" bucket. We report our validation honestly, including a small-sample cluster and a data-quality artifact we chose not to paper over, and close with a ranked action playbook a content reviewer could use today.
Lane: structured content archetype clustering Rows: 30,000 Clusters: 7 Method: K-Means
01

Introduction

A reviewer with limited time needs to know which pages to look at first — and why.

FlyRank's existing content review process leans on a single-dimension rule: an impression_tier that sorts every page into low, moderate, good, or excellent based purely on traffic volume. That rule is fast and easy to explain, but it treats two pages with the same traffic as interchangeable — even when one is thriving and the other is quietly losing ground. This paper asks a narrower, answerable question:

What recurring performance archetypes exist across FlyRank's content, and what review action does each one warrant?

This is decision support, not automation. The output is a ranked short-list for a human reviewer with finite capacity — not a system that edits or removes content on its own, and not a claim about what caused any page's traffic to move.

02

Data

This analysis uses the pseudonymized starter release that ships with the internship repository — 30,000 content pages, each measured over a trailing 90-day window. A parallel data contract was also built and verified against FlyRank's full ~79-million-row warehouse release on Hugging Face (17 months, roughly 70 clients), confirming the same grain, the same signal behavior, and the same data-quality issues described below at scale; the clustering itself here runs on the smaller, faster-to-iterate starter slice.

What's excluded, and why

  • Keyword-research fields (search_volume, competition, cpc) — structurally zero or null for non-keyword content types; including them just re-encodes content type rather than finding real behavior.
  • Precalculated tier columns — redundant bucketed versions of numeric fields already used raw; including both would double-weight the same signal.
  • Content-generation metadata (provider_used, model_used) — not a performance signal.
  • Product decision flags — not present in this dataset at all, by design.

No client names, domains, URLs, or raw queries appear anywhere in this release or in this paper.

The baseline

The transparent baseline this work needs to beat: a page is worth reviewing if it gets real traffic, holds a decent position, and earns noticeably less CTR than other pages at that same position tier — scored as visible × good_position × ctr_gap × impressions, with one reason code (low_ctr_for_position) and one action label (rewrite_title_meta).

Evaluated with precision@K against a declining-page proxy label, this rule scored below the base rate (precision@10 0.300, @20 0.200, @50 0.320, vs. a 0.564 base rate). That's the honest number, not a flattering one — and it makes sense once you look at what the rule actually finds: large, stable or growing pages under-converting their traffic, not pages that are declining. Those are different problems, and the mismatch is worth reporting rather than hiding.

Clustering

K-Means was run on log-transformed traffic and engagement features (impressions, clicks, sessions, users, engaged sessions, AI sessions, scroll events), plus average position, CTR, engagement rate, scroll rate, content age, days since update, and trend percentage — standardized before fitting. Two data-quality issues were corrected first: avg_position == 0 turned out to be a placeholder for "no real position data" rather than an actual top rank (those rows have a median of 1 impression), and trend_pct nulls lined up exactly with a "flat" or "new" trend direction rather than being missing at random.

Silhouette score across candidate k. The curve is fairly flat — k=7 (0.221) narrowly beats k=5 (0.218) and k=6 (0.217) — so this choice is a reasonable one, not a dramatic, obvious elbow.

05

Limitations and honest framing

  • Decision-support only. Nothing here proves a rewrite, refresh, or any other action will change a page's trajectory. That claim would need a real experiment, which this snapshot alone can't provide.
  • One archetype is confounded with a data gap. The "unlabeled specimen" cluster is largely defined by missing word_count, not a clean behavioral pattern — worth investigating at the source before treating it as a genuine archetype.
  • One archetype is too small to trust. The "small-sample outlier" cluster is only 100 pages; its numbers are reported as observed, not as a stable, generalizable pattern.
  • Single snapshot, unbalanced history. Different clients have different amounts of tracking history behind this data, and this analysis reflects one 90-day window, not a trend proven over time.
  • No causal or algorithmic claims. Nothing in this paper describes or predicts how any search engine's ranking algorithm works.

Ranked recommendations

In priority order, for a reviewer with limited capacity:

Flagship in decline (n=2,706) — rewrite candidates. Highest value at risk, steepest decline, weakest engagement.
2
Champion under pressure (n=5,160) — protect and watch closely before it slides further.
3
Unlabeled specimen (n=6,495) — investigate the data-tracking gap at its source before any content action.
4
The steady middle (n=12,187) — standard review queue, no urgency.

Quiet but well-ranked (n=1,140) — monitor; low ceiling limits upside.
